A TurtleBot3 has two wheel encoders and no idea where it is. Every pose it will ever use is one
it computed: the wheels turned by so much, so the robot must have moved by so much. That
sentence hides three problems, and this chapter takes them in order.

The first is arithmetic. A pose in a plane is a position and a heading, and "moved by so much" is
a motion applied *in the robot's own frame*. Add the two the obvious way and the answer is
wrong in a way that does not go away when you shrink the time step. The second is noise: wheels
slip and encoders count in whole ticks, so the same command does not always land in the same
place, and what the robot knows after a move is a spread of places. The third is what to do with
a spread. It can be held as a histogram that assumes nothing, or as two numbers that assume a
great deal, and the chapter ends with one worked example of each.

</Overview>

## The problem

Drive the robot on a circle: forward at $v = 1$ m/s while turning at $\omega = 0.5$ rad/s. Its
path is a circle of radius $v/\omega = 2$ m, and it takes $2\pi/\omega \approx 12.57$ s to come round. The
robot's odometry has to track that path by adding up small motions, one per control step.
The question is how far from the true position it ends up after 12 s, as a function of how long a step
it takes.

Here is the experiment, run on the repository's own Rust. The orange poses are what the robot's
odometry computes, the dark ones are what the textbook update `x += v cos θ Δt` computes, and
the dashed circle is the truth.

<ExactArc />

The orange error is below $10^{-12}$ m at every step length you can set. The dark one is not. At
$\Delta t = 1.5$ s (8 steps) the Euler end point is 0.2134 m from the truth, at $\Delta t = 3$ s
(4 steps) it is 0.4374 m, and halving the step from 1 s to 0.5 s halves the error, from 0.1416 m to
0.0706 m. Euler is not wrong by a random walk or by a drift that gets worse with time; as you
saw, every one of its poses sits on one circle, just not the right one. The rest of this
chapter exists to say *why*, and why the exact version is the one the robot runs.

Wheels bring a second problem. The same command given twice does not give the same pose, and
that is not a flaw of one robot. It is what the next widget shows.

<NoisyArc />

After one command the possible poses are a cloud. After several they are a curved band. Whatever the
robot does next, it does with a *distribution* over where it is, and a pose-with-a-radius is not
a good summary of that distribution. The last two widgets of the chapter hold such a
distribution in the two ways filters usually do.

## Building intuition

Before any algebra, three things to take from the widgets.

**A step is a rotation and a translation at once.** Moving forward while turning does not move
the robot along its old heading and then rotate it; the heading changes *during* the step, so
the direction of travel changes during it too. The exact step is the arc of a circle, and
that is what the orange poses follow. The Euler step goes straight along the *old* heading
and only then turns, so each step is a straight segment in the direction the robot *was* facing, never the direction it was turning through, and its corners cannot land on the circle.

**The error of the cheap step is systematic.** Euler's poses are a regular polygon whose corners
lie on a bigger circle that is shifted sideways. Nothing about it is random, so nothing averages out.

**Noise is not isotropic.** In the second widget, an error in heading early on rotates every
later step, so the error in position is stretched along the arc. A filter that wants to say how
sure it is has to know that, which is why the next chapters work with a covariance in the pose's
own tangent space rather than with a radius.

## The mathematics

### A pose, and how poses combine

A planar pose is $x = (x, y, \theta)$ with $\theta \in (-\pi, \pi]$. It is also a rigid motion: the
one that takes the origin to $(x, y)$ and rotates by $\theta$. Written as a matrix, it is

$$
T = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta & x \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta & y \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

Poses form a group, $\SEtwo$. The product $x_1 x_2$ applies $x_2$ *in the body frame* of $x_1$,
and the inverse $x^{-1}$ undoes $x$. In the repository, that product is `Se2::compose`, the
inverse is `Se2::inverse`, and `Se2::between(a, b)` is $a^{-1}b$: where $b$ is as seen from $a$.

### The exponential map: one exact step

A *twist* $\tau = (v_x, v_y, \omega)$ is a velocity in the body frame, translation first. If the robot holds it
for one unit of time, it moves along the arc the next formula gives, where $a$ and $b$ are two
functions of $\omega$:

$$
\exp(\tau) = \Bigl(a\,v_x - b\,v_y,\; b\,v_x + a\,v_y,\; \omega\Bigr),
\qquad a = \frac{\sin\omega}{\omega},\quad b = \frac{1-\cos\omega}{\omega}.
$$

<Derivation title="Where a and b come from" result="the translation is the integral of the rotating velocity">

Hold the twist for one unit of time. At time $s \in [0,1]$ the heading is $\omega s$, so the
robot is moving in the world frame with velocity $R(\omega s)\,(v_x, v_y)^{\T}$, where $R$ is the
rotation matrix. The position after one unit of time is the integral of that:

$$
\int_0^1 \begin{pmatrix} \cos\omega s & -\sin\omega s \\ \sin\omega s & \cos\omega s \end{pmatrix} ds
\begin{pmatrix} v_x \\ v_y \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} \dfrac{\sin\omega}{\omega} & -\dfrac{1-\cos\omega}{\omega} \\[2mm] \dfrac{1-\cos\omega}{\omega} & \dfrac{\sin\omega}{\omega} \end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} v_x \\ v_y \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} a & -b \\ b & a \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} v_x \\ v_y \end{pmatrix}.
$$

As $\omega \to 0$ the matrix tends to the identity, $a \to 1$ and $b \to 0$, which is straight-line
motion. The repository switches to the series $a \approx 1 - \omega^2/6$, $b \approx \omega/2 - \omega^3/24$
below $|\omega| = 10^{-8}$, because $\sin\omega/\omega$ loses all its digits there.

</Derivation>

A **worked example**: $\exp\bigl((1, 0, \pi/2)\bigr)$ is a quarter circle of radius $2/\pi$, so the robot ends at
$(2/\pi,\, 2/\pi)$, heading $\pi/2$. The formula gives $a = 2/\pi$ and $b = 2/\pi$, so $(a\cdot 1,\, b\cdot 1) = (2/\pi, 2/\pi)$.
The repository pins it as `se2::tests::worked_example_quarter_arc`, and the book's build checks it against the compiled module.

Two names the rest of the book uses. The step $x \bplus \delta = x\,\exp(\delta)$ applies a body-frame twist to a pose,
and $y \bminus x = \log(x^{-1}y)$ is its inverse: the twist that carries $x$ to $y$. One step of a constant velocity
for time $\Delta t$ is therefore

$$
x_{t} = x_{t-1} \bplus (v\,\Delta t,\; 0,\; \omega\,\Delta t),
$$

and it is exact for a robot that really does hold a constant $(v, \omega)$ over the step. That
last condition is the only approximation the step makes, and it does not depend on $\Delta t$.

### Why the Euler step fails, exactly

The Euler step moves along the old heading, then turns: $p \leftarrow p + v\,\Delta t\,(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$, $\theta \leftarrow \theta + \omega\Delta t$.
It is *not* code the robot runs; the repository has it only as the strawman the first widget measures.
Write $\varphi = \omega\,\Delta t$ and positions as complex numbers. Each step adds $v\Delta t\,e^{ik\varphi}$, so after $N$ steps

$$
p_N = v\,\Delta t\,\frac{1 - e^{iN\varphi}}{1 - e^{i\varphi}} .
$$

<Derivation title="All the Euler poses lie on one circle" result="radius r (φ/2) / sin(φ/2), centre shifted from the true centre">

Let $c = v\Delta t/(1 - e^{i\varphi})$. Then $p_N - c = -c\,e^{iN\varphi}$, so $|p_N - c| = |c|$ for every $N$:
all the poses lie on a circle of radius $|c|$. Since $|1 - e^{i\varphi}| = 2\sin(\varphi/2)$,

$$
|c| = \frac{v\,\Delta t}{2\sin(\varphi/2)} = \frac{v}{\omega}\cdot\frac{\varphi/2}{\sin(\varphi/2)} .
$$

The true radius is $v/\omega$, and $(\varphi/2)/\sin(\varphi/2) > 1$ for $\varphi \neq 0$, so the Euler circle is larger.
It is centred at $\bigl(\tfrac{v\Delta t}{2},\, \tfrac{v\Delta t}{2}\cot\tfrac{\varphi}{2}\bigr)$, not at $(0, v/\omega)$. At
$\Delta t = 1.5$ s, $\varphi = 0.75$ and the radius is 2.048 m against the true 2 m. Both circles are
traversed at the same angular rate, so the error is bounded and periodic, and it vanishes as $\Delta t \to 0$
only at first order, which is the proportionality you saw: the book's checks assert every one of these numbers against the closed
form, not against the Rust.

</Derivation>

### From wheels to a twist

The robot's wheels have radius $r$ and sit a distance $\ell$ apart. If the left wheel rolls
an arc $\Delta s_L$ and the right wheel an arc $\Delta s_R$, the body moves forward by their mean and turns by their difference over the track:

$$
\Delta s = \frac{\Delta s_R + \Delta s_L}{2}, \qquad \Delta\theta = \frac{\Delta s_R - \Delta s_L}{\ell},
$$

and the pose changes by $\exp\bigl((\Delta s, 0, \Delta\theta)\bigr)$. A **worked example** with the book's rover Rusty
($\ell = 0.16$ m): the left wheel rolls 0.72 m and the right 0.88 m. Then $\Delta s = 0.80$ m, $\Delta\theta = 1.0$ rad,
and the pose is $(0.673176788,\; 0.367758155,\; 1.0)$. As a check that does not use the code: the arc has radius
$\Delta s/\Delta\theta = 0.8$ m, so $x = 0.8\sin 1$ and $y = 0.8(1 - \cos 1)$, which is the same pair of numbers.
Test `diff_drive::tests::worked_example_wheel_arcs` pins it.

### Noise: the same command, many poses

Real wheels do not do what they are told. The velocity motion model of Thrun, Burgard and Fox (Table 5.3) models
that by perturbing the commanded speeds before taking the exact step. It draws

$$
\hat v = v + \varepsilon_{1},\quad \hat\omega = \omega + \varepsilon_{2},\quad \hat\gamma = \varepsilon_{3},
$$

with zero-mean normal $\varepsilon$ whose variances are $\alpha_1 v^2 + \alpha_2\omega^2$, $\alpha_3 v^2 + \alpha_4\omega^2$ and
$\alpha_5 v^2 + \alpha_6\omega^2$, takes the exact arc at $(\hat v, \hat\omega)$, and then turns by a final $\hat\gamma\,\Delta t$.
The repository's default is $\alpha = (0.02, 0.02, 0.02, 0.02, 0.005, 0.005)$. With every $\alpha = 0$ the model returns the
exact arc: for $v = 1$, $\omega = 0.5$, $\Delta t = 1$ the end pose is
$(2\sin 0.5,\; 2(1 - \cos 0.5),\; 0.5) = (0.958851077208,\; 0.244834876219,\; 0.5)$, which is $\exp((1, 0, 0.5))$
(`velocity::tests::worked_example_exact_arc`). The model is a *sampler*: to know what the robot
might do, draw many poses, as the second widget does.

### Belief, and the Bayes filter

The robot's knowledge of where it is, given everything it has been told and sensed, is a probability
distribution $\bel(x_t)$ over poses. It changes in two ways. A **motion** spreads it out: given the control $u_t$,

$$
\belbar(x_t) = \int p(x_t \mid u_t, x_{t-1})\,\bel(x_{t-1})\,dx_{t-1}.
$$

A **sensor reading** $z_t$ narrows it by Bayes' rule, where $\eta$ is whatever number makes the result sum to one:

$$
\bel(x_t) = \eta\, p(z_t \mid x_t)\,\belbar(x_t).
$$

The number $1/\eta$ is the *evidence*: how likely the reading was before it was seen. A very small
evidence says the filter was surprised, and the repository's `correct` returns it so that the
caller can notice. Both formulas need a representation of $\bel$. The first one is a histogram.

**A worked example.** A 4 m corridor of four 1 m cells, robot position unknown, so the belief is $(0.25, 0.25, 0.25, 0.25)$.
A sensor reports a likelihood of 0.2 in cells 0 and 2 and 0.8 in cells 1 and 3. Multiplying gives $(0.05, 0.2, 0.05, 0.2)$,
which sums to $0.5$, the evidence, and dividing by it gives $\bel = (0.1, 0.4, 0.1, 0.4)$. The test is
`histogram::tests::worked_example_four_cells`. Notice that *two* cells have the highest belief, and
that the belief still puts 20 per cent on the other two.

### The Gaussian belief and the Kalman filter

A histogram over a corridor is 100 numbers. A histogram over $(x, y, \theta)$ at the same resolution is
$100^3 = 10^6$. The alternative is to assume the belief is a Gaussian $\mathcal N(\mu, \Sigma)$ and carry only $\mu$ and $\Sigma$.
If the motion and the measurement are linear and the noises Gaussian, that assumption is not an approximation: both
steps of the Bayes filter map a Gaussian to a Gaussian, and the Kalman filter is their closed form. In one dimension, with
Thrun's naming ($R$ is motion noise, $Q$ is measurement noise), the prediction is

$$
\htmlClass{term-prediction}{\bar\mu = \mu + u}, \qquad \htmlClass{term-prediction}{\bar\Sigma = \Sigma + R},
$$

and the correction multiplies the predicted Gaussian by the measurement's likelihood $\mathcal N(z; x, Q)$. Two Gaussians multiply to a Gaussian,
and the result is

$$
K = \frac{\bar\Sigma}{\bar\Sigma + Q}, \qquad
\htmlClass{term-posterior}{\mu = \bar\mu + K\,(z - \bar\mu)}, \qquad
\htmlClass{term-posterior}{\Sigma = (1 - K)\,\bar\Sigma}.
$$

<Derivation title="The correction is a product of two Gaussians" result="precisions add; the mean is the precision-weighted average">

Up to a constant, $\mathcal N(x; \bar\mu, \bar\Sigma)\,\mathcal N(z; x, Q) \propto \exp\!\bigl(-\tfrac12[(x-\bar\mu)^2/\bar\Sigma + (z-x)^2/Q]\bigr)$.
Collect the terms in $x$: the exponent is $-\tfrac12\bigl[(1/\bar\Sigma + 1/Q)\,x^2 - 2(\bar\mu/\bar\Sigma + z/Q)\,x\bigr] + \text{const}$, which is a Gaussian
with precision $1/\Sigma = 1/\bar\Sigma + 1/Q$ and mean $\mu = \Sigma\,(\bar\mu/\bar\Sigma + z/Q)$. Writing $K = \bar\Sigma/(\bar\Sigma + Q)$,

$$
\Sigma = \frac{\bar\Sigma Q}{\bar\Sigma + Q} = (1 - K)\bar\Sigma, \qquad \mu = \bar\mu + K(z - \bar\mu).
$$

The mean sits at the *precision-weighted* average of the two, so it is halfway between them only if their variances are equal.
Check with the repository's pinned example: $\mathcal N(5, 4)\cdot\mathcal N(6.5, 1)$ has $K = 4/5$, $\mu = 5 + 0.8\cdot 1.5 = 6.2$,
$\Sigma = 0.2\cdot 4 = 0.8$, which is $\mathcal N(6.2, 0.8)$.

</Derivation>

The gain $K$ is the whole story. When the measurement is precise ($Q \ll \bar\Sigma$), $K \to 1$ and the posterior follows
the measurement. When it is noisy ($Q \gg \bar\Sigma$), $K \to 0$ and the posterior ignores it. Nothing in the filter
splits the difference half and half unless $\bar\Sigma = Q$. The repository computes $\Sigma$ not as $(1-K)\bar\Sigma$ but in the *Joseph form*,

$$
\Sigma = (I - KC)\,\bar\Sigma\,(I - KC)^{\T} + K Q K^{\T},
$$

which is algebraically the same and stays symmetric and positive-definite when rounding errors accumulate.

**A worked example** (`kalman::tests::worked_example_1d_cart`): a cart believed to be at $\mathcal N(0, 1)$ is commanded to move $u = 1$
with $R = 0.25$ and then measured at $z = 1.7$ with $Q = 0.5$. The prediction is $\mathcal N(1, 1.25)$; the innovation variance
is $S = \bar\Sigma + Q = 1.75$; the gain is $K = 1.25/1.75 = 5/7$; the posterior is $\mathcal N(1.5,\, 2.5/7)$. The posterior mean is 71 per cent of the way
from the prediction (1) to the measurement (1.7), not 50. Run the filter for a second step ($u = 1$, $z = 2.1$) and it gives $\mu = 2.280645161290323$, $\Sigma = 0.2741935483870968$.

## The algorithm

Two tables in the style of Thrun's, with the symbols of this book, as the repository implements them.

<Algorithm name="BayesFilter(bel, u, z)">

1. predict: $\belbar(x_t) = \int p(x_t \mid u_t, x_{t-1})\,\bel(x_{t-1})\,dx_{t-1}$
2. correct: $\bel(x_t) = \eta\,p(z_t \mid x_t)\,\belbar(x_t)$, with $\eta^{-1} = \int p(z_t \mid x_t)\,\belbar(x_t)\,dx_t$
3. return $\bel(x_t)$ and the evidence $\eta^{-1}$

</Algorithm>

<Algorithm name="KalmanFilter(μ, Σ, u, z)">

1. $\bar\mu = A\mu + Bu$, $\quad\bar\Sigma = A\Sigma A^{\T} + R$
2. $S = C\bar\Sigma C^{\T} + Q$, $\quad K = \bar\Sigma C^{\T} S^{-1}$
3. $\mu = \bar\mu + K(z - C\bar\mu)$
4. $\Sigma = (I - KC)\,\bar\Sigma\,(I - KC)^{\T} + KQK^{\T}$
5. return $\mu$, $\Sigma$, and the evidence $\mathcal N(z;\, C\bar\mu,\, S)$

</Algorithm>

### The pose and its exponential

```rust title="crates/robotics_core/src/se2.rs"
/// A planar pose: translation `(x, y)` in metres and heading `θ` in radians, `θ ∈ (−π, π]`.
///
/// The struct is `Copy`; composing poses does not allocate.
#[derive(Debug, Clone, Copy, PartialEq)]
pub struct Se2 {
    x: f64,
    y: f64,
    theta: f64,
}
// …
    pub fn exp(tau: &Tangent2) -> Self {
        let (vx, vy, w) = (tau[0], tau[1], tau[2]);
        let (a, b) = if w.abs() < SMALL_ANGLE {
            (1.0 - w * w / 6.0, w / 2.0 - w * w * w / 24.0)
        } else {
            let s = (0.5 * w).sin();
            (w.sin() / w, 2.0 * s * s / w)
        };
        Self::new(a * vx - b * vy, b * vx + a * vy, w)
    }
// …
    pub fn compose(&self, other: &Se2) -> Se2 {
        let (s, c) = self.theta.sin_cos();
        Se2::new(
            self.x + c * other.x - s * other.y,
            self.y + s * other.x + c * other.y,
            self.theta + other.theta,
        )
    }
// …
impl Manifold<3> for Se2 {
    #[inline]
    fn boxplus(&self, delta: &SVector<f64, 3>) -> Self {
        self.compose(&Se2::exp(delta))
    }
    #[inline]
    fn boxminus(&self, base: &Self) -> SVector<f64, 3> {
        base.between(self).log()
    }
}
```

`Se2::new` normalizes the heading, so the angle is always in $(-\pi, \pi]$. `exp` is the formula above, with
the series for small $\omega$; `boxplus` is $x \bplus \delta$ and `boxminus` is $y \bminus x$. The trait `Manifold`
is what every filter of later chapters is generic over.

### Wheels to a pose

```rust title="crates/robotics_models/src/diff_drive.rs"
    pub fn wheel_arcs_to_tangent(&self, ds_left: f64, ds_right: f64) -> Tangent2 {
        Tangent2::new(
            0.5 * (ds_right + ds_left),
            0.0,
            (ds_right - ds_left) / self.wheel_separation,
        )
    }
// …
    fn apply(&mut self, delta: Tangent2, dt: f64) -> OdometryStep {
        let step_pose = Se2::exp(&delta);
        // Compound the covariance: the old uncertainty is carried through the new step
        // (Ad of the inverse increment) and the fresh step noise is added.
        let ad_inv = step_pose.inverse().adjoint();
        self.cov = ad_inv * self.cov * ad_inv.transpose() + self.noise.step_covariance(&delta);
        self.pose = self.pose.boxplus(&delta);
```

`WheelOdometry::apply` is the odometry step: it advances the pose by $\bplus\,\Delta$ and, in the same breath, carries the old
covariance through the new step with the adjoint $\Ad$ of the inverse increment and adds the step's own noise. A pose
uncertainty expressed in the body frame has to be rotated into the next body frame, and the adjoint is how. The ROS
node `wheel_odometry_node` reads `joint_states`, calls this, and publishes `/odom`.

One limit belongs here, because the repository states it: the covariance this produces is a simple per-step noise model,
and measured against simulated wheel slip it is over-confident, which `docs/SOURCE_MAPPING.md` (deviation 4)
records and keeps as the raw-odometry baseline. A later chapter measures how over-confident; this one only asks
you not to trust the number as a calibrated probability.

### The noisy step

```rust title="crates/robotics_models/src/motion/velocity.rs"
    /// Table 5.3: `v̂ = v + N(0, α₁v² + α₂ω²)`, `ω̂ = ω + N(0, α₃v² + α₄ω²)`,
    /// `γ̂ = N(0, α₅v² + α₆ω²)`.
    fn sample<R: Rng + ?Sized>(&self, x_prev: &Se2, u: &VelocityCmd, rng: &mut R) -> Se2 {

```

The generator `rng` is passed in. The repository has no entry point that reads the operating system's entropy,
so a run is a function of its seed, and every stochastic widget in this book shows its seed and lets you re-roll it.

### The filters

```rust title="crates/robotics_filters/src/bayes.rs"

    /// The control input type.

    /// The measurement type.
    type Measurement;
// …
    fn predict(&mut self, u: &Self::Control) -> Result<(), FilterError>;

    fn correct(&mut self, z: &Self::Measurement) -> Result<f64, FilterError>;

```

Both methods return a `Result`, which the 2005 book's trait does not (`docs/SOURCE_MAPPING.md`, deviation 2): a
production node must be able to refuse a control or a measurement that is not finite. `correct` returns the evidence.

```rust title="crates/robotics_filters/src/histogram.rs"
    fn correct(&mut self, likelihood: &Self::Measurement) -> Result<f64, FilterError> {
        for i in 0..self.cells.len() {
            let l = likelihood(self.center(i));
            if !l.is_finite() || l < 0.0 {
                return Err(FilterError::NonFinite("likelihood"));
            }
            self.cells[i] *= l;
        }
        self.normalize()
    }
```

That is Bayes' rule on a grid: multiply each cell by the likelihood at its centre, then `normalize`, which returns the sum
it divided by, the evidence. If every cell gets zero likelihood it returns `ZeroLikelihood` instead of a belief that
is not a distribution.

```rust title="crates/robotics_filters/src/kalman.rs"
    fn predict(&mut self, u: &SVector<f64, U>) -> Result<(), FilterError> {
        if u.iter().any(|v| !v.is_finite()) {
            return Err(FilterError::NonFinite("control"));
        }
        let mean = self.a * self.belief.mean() + self.b * u;
        let cov = self.a * self.belief.cov() * self.a.transpose() + self.r;
        self.belief = Gaussian::new(mean, cov)?;
        Ok(())
    }

    fn correct(&mut self, z: &SVector<f64, M>) -> Result<f64, FilterError> {
// …
        let innovation = z - self.c * mu;
        let s = self.c * sigma * self.c.transpose() + self.q;
        let chol = Cholesky::new(s).ok_or(FilterError::SingularInnovation)?;
        let gain = sigma * self.c.transpose() * chol.inverse();
        let mean = mu + gain * innovation;
        let ikc = SMatrix::<f64, N, N>::identity() - gain * self.c;
        let cov = ikc * sigma * ikc.transpose() + gain * self.q * gain.transpose();
        self.belief = Gaussian::new(mean, cov)?;
// …
}
```

The generic `KalmanFilter<N, U, M>` is Table 3.1 in a few dozen lines. Notice that the covariance update is the Joseph form,
and that `Gaussian::new` refuses a covariance that is not positive-definite, so a numerical failure becomes an error at the line that caused it.

## Putting it together

The widget below is the cart of the example, with the gain as the thing to watch.

<KalmanStep />

Now the hallway. A robot that does not know where it starts walks a 10 m corridor with three identical doors and asks a
door detector, right 90 per cent of the time, after every 0.5 m. A `HistogramFilter1D` of 100 cells tracks it.

<Hallway />

After the first "door" reading from the uniform belief, the belief has three equal bumps, each holding 22 per cent of
the mass; the sensor cannot tell the doors apart, and that is a statement about the sensor, not a failure of the filter. Only later readings, with
motion in between them, tell the doors apart. The filter's most probable cell is a poor answer to "where is the robot" whenever the belief has more
than one bump, and a mean is no better: the mean of two bumps sits between them, where the robot is not. A belief is not a point.

Here is the problem of the chapter, restated as numbers.

- **The step.** The exact step ends within $10^{-12}$ m of the true pose at every step length, and the Euler step is 0.2134 m off at $\Delta t = 1.5$ s.
- **The noise.** The same command gives a cloud, and the cloud bends along the arc.
- **The belief.** A histogram assumes nothing and costs a cell per resolution element; with three coordinates it costs a million. A Kalman filter costs two numbers per dimension and is exact only when the world is linear and Gaussian, which a pose is not: it lives on a curved space, and the next chapters' filters work on its tangent space for exactly that reason.

What this chapter does *not* show, and the repository says it does not: the odometry covariance of Part 1 is not calibrated, the
histogram filter is one-dimensional, and the Kalman filter in the widget is the scalar case. The first is measured in a later chapter, and the other two are
the reason a later chapter exists.

<Exercise level="F" difficulty={2} title="One circle, which radius">

Show that the Euler poses of the first widget lie on a single circle and find its radius in terms of $v$, $\omega$ and $\Delta t$.
Which direction is its centre shifted from the true centre $(0, v/\omega)$?

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

With $\varphi = \omega\Delta t$, $p_N = v\Delta t\,(1 - e^{iN\varphi})/(1 - e^{i\varphi})$ is a geometric sum. Put $c = v\Delta t/(1 - e^{i\varphi})$: then
$p_N - c = -c\,e^{iN\varphi}$ and $|p_N - c| = |c|$, a constant. Since $|1 - e^{i\varphi}| = 2\sin(\varphi/2)$ the radius is $v\Delta t/(2\sin(\varphi/2))$.
The centre is $c = \bigl(\tfrac{v\Delta t}{2}, \tfrac{v\Delta t}{2}\cot\tfrac{\varphi}{2}\bigr)$, which has a positive $x$ component: shifted to the right in the widget.

</details>

</Exercise>

<Exercise level="F" difficulty={2} title="The gain from scratch">

Derive $K = \bar\Sigma/(\bar\Sigma + Q)$ and $\Sigma = (1 - K)\bar\Sigma$ by multiplying the two Gaussians of the scalar correction, and evaluate them
for $\bar\Sigma = 1.25$, $Q = 0.5$.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

Add the precisions: $1/\Sigma = 1/\bar\Sigma + 1/Q$, so $\Sigma = \bar\Sigma Q/(\bar\Sigma + Q)$. The mean is $\Sigma(\bar\mu/\bar\Sigma + z/Q) = \bar\mu + K(z - \bar\mu)$ with
$K = \Sigma/Q = \bar\Sigma/(\bar\Sigma + Q)$. For the numbers given, $K = 1.25/1.75 = 5/7$ and $\Sigma = (2/7)(1.25) = 2.5/7$.

</details>

</Exercise>
